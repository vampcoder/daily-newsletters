Over the past few weeks, I’ve been writing to you about how our beliefs and the stories we tell ourselves influence what we do and what we think we’re capable of.

After spending the last few months talking with readers (on calls, in events, and through your replies), one theme has come up repeatedly: Even when we understand what’s good for us and believe we can act on it, why don’t we always follow through?

It’s a very common challenge and something I’ve been facing in my own life as well.

During my recent book launch, my schedule filled with messages and requests: podcast invitations, bookstore events, press interviews. All of it was meaningful, and in many ways extremely energizing.

At the same time, I was going to bed later than I had planned, postponing workouts, and even giving writing, which I value deeply, the back seat.

No singular choice felt particularly worrisome, but taken together, they moved me away from what I had intended.

For a long time, I assumed that if we could just try a little harder or organize things a bit more effectively, the problem would resolve itself. But that hasn’t been my experience, or the experience of my readers over the years.

What I’ve found instead is that the challenge often begins with small, uncomfortable states: a sense of unease, fatigue, lingering stress. These feelings are easy to overlook, yet they heavily influence what we do next.

If you’ve read *Indistractable*, you may remember that distraction doesn’t begin with our devices. It begins with the discomfort we’re trying to avoid.

The more clearly we can see those moments, the easier it becomes to change how we respond to them.
