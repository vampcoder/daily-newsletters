Recently a reader asked me how to stay motivated to do the work after being dealt a number of setbacks. How do you keep studying when you've failed the exam three times already? How do you keep sending resumes when you've been turned down after each interview? How do you persist when all you experience is failure?

This is a problem I've thought a lot about, both intellectually and personally.

Intellectually, the role of setbacks in motivation is well-understood. Progress is one of the most reliable motivators in our work. And setbacks aren't just negative progress—[research by Teresa Amabile](https://www.amazon.ca/Progress-Principle-Ignite-Engagement-Creativity/dp/142219857X/) finds that setback experiences are roughly three times as negative as progress is positive.

Similarly, it's long been understood in therapeutic contexts that [lacking self-efficacy](https://www.amazon.com/Social-Learning-Theory-Albert-Bandura/dp/0138167443) can create a self-fulfilling prophecy: when we have an irrational fear, we avoid exposing ourselves to scary situations, even if they might provide evidence our fears are overblown. Likewise, if we feel like everything we try ends up in failure, we don't put in the effort to make it succeed.

I've been there. I've experienced many moments of feeling completely stuck, where everything I try doesn't seem to work. It took me over five years writing before I found a way to make a living from it. My motivation to keep going was often mixed in with a considerable amount of self-doubt.

Thus, even beyond an intellectual interest, trying to resolve the problem of motivation in the face of setbacks has had enormous practical consequences for my life, as I'm sure it does for everyone.

## Is Giving Up Actually Irrational?

A lot of self-improvement advice takes the perspective that persistence is an unqualified good. In this view, staying motivated is hard, but it's always the right decision when faced with challenges in life.

Frankly, this is false. Giving up is the correct answer in many situations. If anything, human beings have a tendency to persist too long in many pursuits. We fail to exclude [sunk costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunk_cost) when evaluating our decisions. We allow initial conditions to [frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anchoring_effect) our future decisions. We take unnecessary gambles to [win back losses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prospect_theory). In many situations, then, *quitting* is the right move.
