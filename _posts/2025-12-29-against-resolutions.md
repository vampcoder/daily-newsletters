# Against Resolutions

**Suleika Jaouad on ritual, repetition, and the fantasy of starting over**

Every year, we are told that a new year means a new you. We make lists of resolutions — lose weight, save money, read more, stress less — as if turning the calendar page can erase the past and deliver us, reborn, into a future of our own design. But this fantasy of starting over is not only unrealistic; it can be a trap.

I have spent much of my life in transition: from diagnosis to treatment, from treatment to remission, from the hospital to the world outside. Each time I thought I could leave my old self behind, I learned that life does not work that way. We carry our history with us — in our bodies, in our habits, in the relationships that shape us. The desire to start over is human, but it often leads us to neglect the very practices that could sustain us.

What I have come to trust instead is ritual. Not the kind that is grandiose or spiritual for display, but the small, repeated acts that ground us: making coffee in the morning, walking the same path, writing in a notebook before bed. Ritual is not about novelty; it is about return. It tells us that meaning is found not in the dramatic reboot but in the steady, quiet rhythm of showing up.

Repetition can be dismissed as boring, but I have found it to be a form of devotion. When I was sick, my days were punctuated by rituals — the port flush, the pill box, the prayer I never knew I believed in. They did not promise a cure, but they gave me a way to live inside uncertainty. They were a way of saying: *I am still here, and I will continue.*

So this year, I am not making resolutions. I am renewing my commitments to the rituals that have carried me through. I will light the same candle each morning. I will write to a friend each week. I will walk to the river and listen. Nothing about this is exciting or new — and that is precisely the point. The new is not ahead of us; it is in the way we return to what matters.

As you face the turning of the year, I invite you to ask: What ritual can you return to? Not a goal to achieve, but a practice to keep. Let it be small, let it be repeatable, let it be yours. And in that repetition, you may find not the fantasy of starting over, but the reality of beginning again — and again, and again.
