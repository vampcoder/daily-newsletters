In 1923, a man named Edoardo Agnelli did something unusual for an Italian industrialist. His father had founded Fiat — Italy’s largest car company. The family was already one of the wealthiest in Europe. But Edoardo didn’t buy another factory or expand into a new market. He bought a football club in Turin called Juventus.

At the time, it seemed like a vanity purchase. But that decision turned Juventus into one of the most successful football clubs in the world, and it made the Agnelli family synonymous with Italian football for generations. The club became a cultural institution, a source of soft power, and eventually a profitable business in its own right.

Fast forward a century, and the pattern has repeated across the globe. From wealthy Indian families buying cricket franchises in the IPL to private equity firms acquiring football clubs in Europe, the rich are increasingly drawn to sports team ownership. Why?

One reason is scarcity. There are only a limited number of major sports franchises in the world, and they rarely come up for sale. Unlike most assets, sports teams tend to appreciate in value regardless of on-field performance. Even losing teams often see their valuations rise due to league-wide broadcasting deals, sponsorship growth, and the sheer prestige of ownership.

Another factor is access. Owning a team grants entry into an exclusive network of billionaires, politicians, and celebrities. It opens doors that money alone sometimes cannot. For many owners, the business relationships fostered through team ownership far outweigh the direct financial returns.

Then there’s the emotional and cultural dimension. A sports franchise is a community asset. It carries history, passion, and loyalty. For wealthy families, owning a team is a way to build a legacy that transcends business. It’s a long-term investment in identity — for themselves and their descendants.

But it’s not just about prestige. Modern sports ownership has become more sophisticated. Data analytics, global marketing, and revenue diversification have turned franchises into multi-faceted businesses. Owners can now monetize everything from merchandise to media rights to real estate development around stadiums.

Still, the economics can be tricky. Operating costs are high, and returns are often tied to league-wide success. For every Juventus, there are clubs that struggle financially. Yet the allure remains.

Perhaps the clearest lesson comes from the Agnelli family. They understood that a sports team is more than a business — it’s a platform. In the years since, that insight has guided the investment decisions of countless wealthy individuals and dynasties around the world.

As sports continue to globalize, the competition for ownership will only intensify. The result? The rich will keep buying sports teams, not just because they can, but because it’s one of the few assets that offers a blend of financial upside, social access, and lasting legacy.
