Last week, I talked about [my process](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2025/09/09/knowledge-sprint/) for doing intensive research combined with building new habits that I’ve been using this past year in my Foundations project. One question that sometimes comes up when I emphasize the value of doing research is whether reading a lot of books is necessary. Did you really need to read books to tell you that exercising was important? Or that you shouldn’t eat junk food? That you should get to bed on time?

I think this “I know that already” problem infects a lot of areas of self-improvement. In brief:

1. **Hindsight bias is strong.** [Everything is obvious](https://www.amazon.ca/Everything-Obvious-Common-Sense-Fails/dp/0307951790) once you know the answer. The value of research is usually in telling you which of two plausible theories is true, or which advice from different, seemingly sensible approaches works best.
2. **Reading is attentional, not just informational.** My [heavy](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2024/10/22/what-i-learned-about-getting-in-shape-after-reading-13-books-this-month/) [reading](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2024/11/19/10-important-books-productivity/) [lists](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2024/12/17/10-books-money/) [didn’t](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2025/01/13/what-i-learned-and-unlearned-reading-10-books-on-nutrition/) [just](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2025/02/25/reflections-on-a-month-for-reading/) [teach](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2025/03/18/8-books-on-making-friends/) [me](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2025/04/22/sleep-books/) [new](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2025/05/20/reflection-books/) [things](https://www.scotthyoung.com/blog/2025/06/17/reflection-books/). They also build sustained attention, a skill that’s increasingly rare and valuable. Reading, especially long-form, trains the mind to hold complex ideas and follow nuanced arguments. That’s how you develop judgment, not just information.

But the bigger point is that even when I already knew the broad strokes, the details mattered. For example, I knew exercise was good for me, but I didn’t fully appreciate the crucial distinction between aerobic and resistance training. I knew sleep mattered, but I didn’t understand how much sleep debt impacts cognitive function. I knew nutrition affected health, but I was surprised to learn how context-dependent the advice is. Research doesn’t just tell you what to do; it explains why a particular approach works and where it fails.

With that preface, here are 12 things I changed my mind about after researching for Foundations:

1. **I changed my mind about the importance of strength training.** I used to think of strength training as optional, but the evidence is clear: it’s essential for long-term health, metabolic function, and longevity. You don’t need to be a bodybuilder, but you need to lift weights or do resistance exercises at least twice a week.

2. **I changed my mind about cardio.** I thought cardio was the best way to stay healthy, but I now see that it’s just one piece of the puzzle. High-intensity interval training (HIIT) can yield most of the cardiovascular benefits in much less time, which is a practical advantage for busy people.

3. **I changed my mind about stretching.** I used to believe stretching prevented injury and soreness, but the research is mixed. Static stretching before exercise may actually impair performance. The key is dynamic warm-ups, not static stretching. That’s a nuance I didn’t know before.

4. **I changed my mind about steps.** I thought walking 10,000 steps a day was a marketing gimmick, and while the exact number is arbitrary, the evidence is clear: more daily movement is associated with lower mortality and better cardiovascular health. Even if the specific target is flexible, the principle is solid.

5. **I changed my mind about diet.** I used to be more flexible about diet, but the research strongly supports a diet high in whole foods, vegetables, and lean proteins, and low in ultra-processed foods. It’s not about cutting everything you enjoy, but the balance matters a lot.

6. **I changed my mind about supplements.** I used to think supplements were a waste of money, but I now see that some are evidence-based and can be beneficial, like vitamin D in winter or creatine for strength. But the majority are hype. The key is to distinguish between them.

7. **I changed my mind about sleep.** I always valued sleep, but I didn’t realize how much sleep debt affects everything from immune function to mood and memory. I now treat sleep as non-negotiable, not as a luxury that can be skimped on.

8. **I changed my mind about stress management.** I used to think stress management was a soft skill, but it has concrete physiological effects. Chronic stress increases cortisol, which alters metabolism and mood. I now see that stress reduction isn’t optional—it’s a health intervention.

9. **I changed my mind about social connections.** I didn’t expect this to be a core topic, but the evidence is overwhelming: loneliness is as damaging as smoking. I now prioritize social time as a health behavior, not just a personal preference.

10. **I changed my mind about happiness.** I thought happiness was a personality trait, but research shows you can increase it with deliberate practices like gratitude journaling and savoring. It’s not just about changing circumstances, but also about changing how you process experience.

11. **I changed my mind about memory.** I used to think memory was a natural gift, but the research shows that memory techniques like spaced repetition and memory palaces are trainable. The key is to practice and use the right cues.

12. **I changed my mind about learning.** I always loved learning, but I now realize that effective learning is a skill that requires deliberate practice, reflection, and making connections. It’s not just about absorbing information; it’s about constructing understanding.

These aren’t just intellectual insights. I’ve been applying them, and they’ve changed how I work, exercise, eat, sleep, and interact with people. And that’s the real value of research: it doesn’t just tell you facts, it changes what you do.
