## Editor's Picks: Closing auction confusion

**August 03, 2026**

If you have ever watched the stock market in the last five minutes of trading, you know the drill. The index swings wildly, individual stocks spike or crash, and then—just as suddenly—everything settles. That is the closing auction: a brief and critical window that determines the day's official closing price. But a string of recent episodes has left many investors puzzled, and the regulator is taking notice.

The problem is not new. For years, market participants have complained that the closing price is often out of sync with the day's overall trend. The reason lies in the mechanics of the auction itself. Unlike regular trading, where orders match continuously, the closing session collects orders for a short period and then matches them all at a single price. This design is meant to bring transparency and efficiency, but it also creates an opportunity for a handful of large trades to move the price disproportionately.

Consider what happened last month. On several days, the Nifty closed nearly a hundred points away from where it had been trading just minutes before. Retail investors, who had placed market orders during the day, found their fills at prices that felt disconnected from the broader session. The finger-pointing was immediate, but the real culprit was the structure of the auction itself.

The Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) has responded with a set of measures. The closing session will be extended to thirty minutes, and the exact end time will be randomized to make it harder for traders to time their big orders. There is also talk of limiting the price range for orders placed in the final seconds. On paper, these steps make sense. They reduce the predictability that market manipulators exploit. But will they be enough?

Not if the underlying liquidity problem remains. The closing auction is thin by nature—most of the day's volume happens in continuous trading. That means even a modest order can have an outsized effect. Extending the session may encourage more participation, but it will not guarantee it. And randomizing the end time, while clever, does not address the core issue: the market is still vulnerable to a single large player who decides to dump or buy aggressively at the close.

Some market experts argue that the real fix lies in increasing the minimum lot size or in creating incentives for institutional investors to participate in the closing session. Others suggest a more radical approach: moving to a closing price that is calculated as a volume-weighted average of the last half-hour of trading, rather than a single auction. That would smooth out the spikes and make the close less prone to distortion.

Until then, traders and investors would do well to be cautious. If you are placing orders in the final minutes, known that you are operating in a market that is both volatile and opaque. It may be safer to avoid market orders at the close altogether and use limit orders instead. Or simply wait for the next day's open, when liquidity returns and the price is more reflective of genuine supply and demand.

The closing auction is a puzzle that has confounded market watchers for years. Its mystery is not just academic—it affects the prices you see, the performance of your portfolio, and the integrity of the market as a whole. As SEBI rolls out its reforms, the hope is that the confusion will finally subside. But given the complexity of the issue, the only certainty is that the debate is far from over.
