# Civilization on the Electronic Frontier

**An essay from our cofounder Hamish McKenzie on the internet's original promise, and how we might still realize it.**

## I. Riding with the cowboy poet

On February 8, 1996, as President Bill Clinton signed the Communications Decency Act into law, John Perry Barlow mounted a resistance. The Wyoming cattle farmer, technologist, and poet published [A Declaration of the Independence of Cyberspace](https://substack.com/redirect/34675d8c-867d-49c7-b91c-3a26d6b75db5?j=eyJ1IjoiM3ZldWEifQ.GVk46KgTB2RDl8XiyIYsnzgCOJGT_g7PXHdsRNh4piw), declaring the worldwide web a haven from governments that seek to put constraints on human thought. "We are creating a world where anyone, anywhere may express his or her beliefs, no matter how singular, without fear of being coerced into silence or conformity," he wrote.

Barlow, best known as a lyricist for the Grateful Dead, had believed in the promise of digital technology since the 1980s, when he found that an Apple Macintosh made it easier to run the 22,000-acre ranch on which he had been raised. As an early member of the online bulletin board [The WELL](https://substack.com/redirect/a078e911-6896-4cd7-b223-33152b536866?j=eyJ1IjoiM3ZldWEifQ.GVk46KgTB2RDl8XiyIYsnzgCOJGT_g7PXHdsRNh4piw), he came to understand the power of digital communities to connect individuals across distances, and to foster a new kind of civic space.
