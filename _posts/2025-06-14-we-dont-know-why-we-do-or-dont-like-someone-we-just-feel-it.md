## In this edition: the Zynnification of culture, Andy Warhol’s cookbook, and the algorithm of attraction

A curious thing is happening online. Platforms that once rewarded deep engagement are shifting toward what some call the “Zynnification” of culture—a reference to the short-video app Zynn, which paid users for watching content. The pattern is now everywhere: everything is accelerated, gamified, and optimized for the quick hit. We scroll, we like, we swipe, and we move on.

Nowhere is this more visible than in the realm of romance. Dating apps have become the norm, and with them, a new kind of human interaction—one governed by algorithms that promise to sort through millions of faces to find “the one.” But as we swipe left or right in a split second, we are training ourselves to make snap judgments about people. We don’t know why we do or don’t like someone. We just feel it.

The implications are profound. If we’re outsourcing our instincts to a machine, are we losing something essential about how we connect with others? Perhaps we are. The speed of these exchanges leaves little room for the slow, messy, and often surprising process of getting to know someone. We see a profile, a photo, a few lines of text, and we decide within seconds.

But this isn’t just about dating. It’s about all of our interactions. The same quick-judgment logic is being applied to news, to art, to culture. We consume and discard with unprecedented speed. The result is a world that feels thinner, less substantial.

There are, however, countermovements. People are seeking out analog experiences, nostalgic formats, even cookbooks from icons like Andy Warhol—an artist who, despite his fascination with consumer culture, found meaning in the tangible act of making and sharing food. Warhol’s cookbook is a reminder that some things are best done slowly, with intention, and in the company of others.

In a culture that’s accelerating, the radical act may be to slow down. To let ourselves sit with uncertainty, to resist the urge to judge quickly, and to embrace the complexity of human connection.
