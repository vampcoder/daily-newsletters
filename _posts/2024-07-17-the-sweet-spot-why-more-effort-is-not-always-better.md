I recently finished listening to Rich Roll's memoir, *Finding Ultra*, on audiobook. Hearing about a guy who completed five Ironman triathlons in a single week is great motivation to not wuss out when you're in the middle of a run.

But while Roll's story is undoubtedly inspiring, it got me thinking about a central question in my pursuit of building better foundations: how much is good enough?

Fitness, like most foundations, has ample benefits. It makes you healthier, increases energy, boosts cognition and helps you live longer. Yet it seems our brains didn't evolve to make us like to exercise, so as a society, we're chronically out-of-shape.

The benefits of exercise form a classic diminishing returns curve. People who do zero exercise benefit tremendously from moving a little. People who meet recommended minimums still benefit from exercising more, but it's a smaller improvement than going from no exercise to some. The common advice amongst fitness advocates seems to be, "Some exercise is good; more is better."

But there's more to life than just staying in shape.

Even Roll, for all his incredible accomplishments, noted many of the drawbacks his intensive training initially generated: his focus on training led to the decline of his legal practice, putting his family through financial challenges, and his wife had to manage a larger share of the household work. Roll turned his success into a best-selling book and popular podcast. But for the rest of us, fitness is a side-hustle, not a career.

The question isn't limited to exercise. Several years ago, I went on a ten-day meditation retreat. When the retreat finished, it was impressed upon us that the way to maintain our newfound mental clarity was to meditate for two hours every single day.

How do you get the balance right? How do you decide how much to invest in a foundation, and when do you decide you're already doing enough?

Here's The Wrong Way to Answer This Question

Let's start with how we typically make decisions of this kind: we look around us, see what other people are doing, and make a decision based on what kind of investment seems "normal."

Delegating decisions of this kind can be adaptive in many areas of life. The cultural evolution hypothesis argues that cultures can adopt beneficial practices without necessarily having a good rational understanding of why they work. We can benefit from the wisdom embedded in cultural norms without having to figure everything out from scratch.

But this approach has a problem: what's normal can be far from optimal. The people we compare ourselves to are often the most extreme examples, not the typical ones. We see a fitness influencer training for an Ironman and assume we should be doing more. We hear about a meditation guru who meditates for hours a day and feel guilty about our twenty-minute practice. The result is that we often end up feeling inadequate, pushing ourselves toward levels of effort that give us very little extra benefit while draining time and energy that could be spent on other pursuits.

The Right Way: Marginal Thinking

Instead of asking "What do others do?" we should ask "What do I gain from doing a little more?" This is the logic of marginal benefits. The first block of effort—the one that takes you from zero to something—is typically huge. The next block, less so. The block after that, even less.

If you're a busy parent with limited time, adding a fifth hour of exercise per week might not bring much additional benefit in health, but it might cost you valuable time with your kids. On the other hand, a professional athlete might find that extra hour gives them the edge they need to win.

The key is to weigh the benefit of another unit of effort against the cost. When the cost starts to outweigh the benefit, you've found your sweet spot.

This isn't an argument for doing the bare minimum. It's an argument for being intentional. The goal is to get the biggest payoff from your foundations without letting them consume your life. That requires recognizing that at some point, more effort stops giving you a meaningful return, and that's okay.

In practice, this means setting a baseline that is good enough for your goals, then occasionally re-evaluating as circumstances change. It also means being honest with yourself about your true objectives. If you exercise to feel good and stay healthy, then a reasonable routine that you enjoy is probably enough. If you're training for a race, you'll need to do more.

Ultimately, the question "how much is good enough?" is not a cop-out. It's the most important question you can ask about any foundation. Because if you don't ask it, you'll either do too little and miss out on the benefits, or do too much and miss out on the rest of life.

So the next time you find yourself feeling inadequate because you're not doing as much as the extreme examples around you, pause. Ask yourself: What would another hour actually get me? The answer might surprise you.
