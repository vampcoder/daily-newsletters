I recently had a minor "digital crisis" that many of you will find familiar. I was trying to record a quick video on my phone, only to be met with the dreaded: "Storage almost full."

I didn't delete photos. I didn't transfer files to a hard drive. I simply tapped a button to upgrade my iCloud storage for a few hundred rupees a month.

Later that evening at the office, I was chatting with a few younger team members about random expenses. At some point, we started listing the things they pay for every month: Netflix, Prime, JioHotstar, Spotify, YouTube Premium, iCloud, and a handful of fitness and meditation apps. The numbers were startling. For someone in their mid-20s, these small charges added up to nearly ₹50,000 a year.

But the bigger realisation came when we did the math on what these subscriptions would cost over a lifetime. If you start paying ₹5,000 a month on subscriptions at age 25 and continue until you're 60, that's a crore-and-a-half of your hard-earned money. And what do you own at the end of it? Nothing. No asset. No equity. No residual value.

The subscription model is designed to feel painless. A few hundred rupees a month doesn't sting like a single lakh-rupee purchase. But the compounding effect on your wealth is enormous. While these services provide convenience and entertainment, they also represent a continuous outflow of money that could have been invested or used to build assets.

The problem isn't that subscriptions are inherently bad. It's that most of us have lost track of them. We don't think of them as a monthly cost. We think of them as "just a few hundred rupees". But over a decade, those few hundred rupees compound into lakhs.

Take a simple example. A premium music streaming service costs about ₹1,200 a year. Over 30 years, that's roughly ₹36,000. Now, if that same money is invested and earns an 11% return (the long-term average of a good equity portfolio), it can grow to more than ₹2.5 lakh at retirement. That's the opportunity cost of a single subscription.

Now multiply that across all the subscriptions you hold. The cumulative loss is staggering.

I'm not suggesting you cancel everything and live like a hermit. I'm suggesting you become intentional.

Here are a few things you can do today:

1. **Audit every active subscription.** Look at your bank and credit card statements. Identify every recurring charge. You’ll be surprised.

2. **Categorise them.** Which ones truly add value to your life? Which ones do you not even remember using last month?

3. **Cancel the noise.** Keep only the subscriptions you genuinely use and derive real benefit from. For the rest, cancel them right away.

4. **Re-route the money.** Redirect the amount you save from cancelled subscriptions into an investment plan. Even a small amount, invested consistently, can make a real difference over time.

The subscription economy is a subtle, silent force. It doesn't demand your attention like a big purchase does. But it's eating away at your future wealth, one small monthly charge at a time.

The point isn't to stop spending on things you love. It's to make sure your money is working for you, not just streaming out of your account.

So, what are you paying for every month that you don't even think about? And more importantly, what could that money do for you if it were invested instead?
