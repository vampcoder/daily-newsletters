Welcome back to my little science corner, Amol.

This month, three studies stopped me in my tracks, because they answered some very interesting questions:

1. Can spending more time in daylight, rather than artificial light, help our glucose?
2. Is skipping breakfast healthy?
3. Does having protein before bed help us sleep better?

Let me walk you through what the scientists actually did, what they found, and what it means for our everyday lives.

**Study #1: Daylight and your glucose**

Researchers were curious about the effect of daylight on glucose, so they gave 13 people with type 2 diabetes two very different offices to work in. One had big windows and natural daylight. The other had no windows at all, only electrical light.

Everyone worked a whole working week in each office, in a random order, so each person was their own point of comparison. Their glucose was measured continuously throughout.

The results: Under natural daylight, they spent almost **2 extra hours per day in a healthier glucose range** (50.9% vs 43.3% of their time in normal glucose range). Their bodies also leaned more on fat for energy, rather than sugar.

What this means for you: Getting outside during the day, even just for 10–15 minutes after a meal, can help your glucose stay flatter and your metabolism work more efficiently. Natural light sets your circadian rhythm, which in turn helps your body use insulin better.

**Study #2: Is skipping breakfast healthy?**

We're often told that intermittent fasting is good for us, but not all fasting is equal. Researchers wanted to look at how skipping breakfast specifically affects our metabolism.

In this study, participants were divided into two groups: one ate breakfast, the other skipped it, but both consumed the same amount of calories and nutrients throughout the day. Blood samples were taken frequently, and their glucose and insulin were carefully monitored.

What they found was striking: the group that skipped breakfast had significantly higher blood sugar spikes after lunch and dinner, and their insulin-response stayed elevated for longer. Over time, this pattern can increase the risk of insulin resistance and type 2 diabetes.

What this means for you: If you're someone who skips breakfast, you might want to reconsider. Eating a balanced breakfast—especially with protein—can help stabilize your glucose for the rest of the day and reduce the chance of those afternoon energy crashes.

**Study #3: Protein before bed**

For years, we've heard conflicting advice about eating before bed. This study looked specifically at a small protein snack before sleep.

Participants consumed either a protein-rich pudding or a placebo before bed, and their sleep was recorded with sophisticated EEG monitors. The results were impressive: those who had protein before bed experienced deeper, more restorative sleep, with fewer nighttime awakenings and better glucose control overnight.

The reason? Protein provides a steady supply of amino acids, which help repair muscles and produce satisfying neurotransmitters. It also prevents overnight glucose dips, which can wake you up or disturb your sleep cycles.

What this means for you: If you find yourself waking up in the middle of the night or craving sweet things the next morning, try a small protein snack—like cottage cheese or Greek yogurt—about an hour before bed. Aim for around a palm-sized portion to avoid overeating.

A quick note: these studies are small, but they're part of a growing body of evidence that our daily routines—not just what we eat—deeply affect our metabolic health. Try one of these changes for a week and see how you feel.
