For much of the last century, careers followed a familiar trajectory. You worked hard through your 20s, built stability in your 30s, chased success in your 40s, and only then, if all went well, earned the bragging rights to pause.

But now, that rhythm of life — work, save, retire, feels increasingly outdated.

This Diwali break gave me a chance to slow down and spend time with family, something our schedules rarely allow. Between conversations, celebrations, and a bit of quiet, I found myself thinking about how we plan our work and careers, but how little we plan our rest.

Fortunately, more and more people today are changing that. They are not waiting until sixty to live deliberately. They are planning multiple "mini-retirements" through life, structured breaks of six months to a year to travel, upskill, write, explore, or simply breathe.

Unlike a career break taken out of exhaustion, a mini-retirement is planned in advance, financially, mentally, and professionally. The idea revolves around pausing with purpose. For some, it means stepping away to pursue a personal project. For others, it is time spent learning, caring for family, or simply resetting pace.

It's the new talking point in every high-performing circle and the lifestyle trend everyone seems curious about. You hear it over coffee, at offsites, even in investor meetings — someone is always planning a time off, or just coming back from one.

In this edition, we'll look at:

- The new rhythm of work
- The economics of time
- The financial readiness checklist
- Making your comeback count

Let's dive in.

## The new rhythm of work

Work has always been the centre of gravity for most of us. It gives structure, purpose, and pride. That hasn't changed. What's changed is the environment around it.

Technology has made us more productive than ever, but also permanently reachable. The day starts earlier, ends later, and follows us into weekends and vacations. The lines between office and home have blurred, and with them, the boundaries that once made retirement feel like a release.

At the same time, the idea of a single, linear career is fading. People switch industries, start side projects, and take sabbaticals in ways that would have seemed risky a generation ago. The companies that thrive, and the individuals who flourish, are those that treat a career as a series of seasons, not a single long march.

Mini-retirements fit naturally into this new rhythm. They are not an escape from work, but a recalibration of it. A deliberate pause that allows you to return with more energy, clarity, and often, a deeper sense of what you actually want from the next stage.

The challenge, of course, is that this runs against decades of conditioning. We are taught to equate rest with laziness, and busyness with worth. But the most successful people I know are not the ones who never stop; they are the ones who know when to press pause.

## The economics of time

Time is the one asset you can't earn more of. Yet most of us treat it as if it's infinite, deferring the best of it to a future that may never come.

Mini-retirements force you to confront this trade-off head-on. They ask: What are you working for? What are you deferring, and is the deferral worth it?

The economics of time are simple. You exchange a certain number of working years for a certain quality of life. If you compress all your living into the end, you may end up with a comfortable retirement but a life that was mostly spent waiting for it. Mini-retirements spread the payoff across the journey, making each phase of life worth living in itself.

Financially, they require a different kind of planning. Not just saving for a retirement that's decades away, but creating the flexibility to step away sooner. This means building a buffer, whether it's an emergency fund, a portfolio of passive income, or simply living below your means so you can afford a year without a salary.

The key is to think in periods, not in milestones. Instead of asking, "Can I afford to retire?" ask "Can I afford to take six months off?" The first is a binary, all-or-nothing question. The second is a design problem you can solve.

## The financial readiness checklist

Before you plan a mini-retirement, you need a clear picture of your finances. Here is a practical checklist.

1. **Know your runway.** Calculate your monthly expenses and multiply by the number of months you plan to take off. Add a buffer of at least 20% for surprises.

2. **Fund the break, not the career.** A mini-retirement is a temporary pause, not an exit. You don't need to replace your entire income — just cover your expenses during the gap.

3. **Pay off high-interest debt.** The last thing you want during a break is the stress of credit card bills or loans eating into your runway.

4. **Keep an emergency fund separate.** Your mini-retirement fund and your emergency fund should not be the same. An emergency fund covers life's surprises; the mini-retirement fund pays for the surprise you've chosen.

5. **Understand your health insurance.** If your employer pays for your coverage, find out how you'll stay insured during the break.

6. **Plan your re-entry.** Map out how you'll return to work, whether that's a job, freelance, or a business. A mini-retirement is a sabbatical, not a disconnection.

## Making your comeback count

The best mini-retirements end with a plan. You don't need to have your next job lined up on day one, but you should have a sense of what you're coming back to.

Use the time to sharpen your skills, explore adjacent fields, or just restore your mental bandwidth. The clarity you gain is often more valuable than the break itself. When you return, you'll bring more than a tan — you'll bring perspective.

Some people find they don't want to return to the same career path at all. That's fine. A mini-retirement can be the pause that resets your trajectory.

What matters is that you treat the return as part of the plan, not an afterthought. Because the goal is not to escape work forever. It's to build a life where work, rest, and meaning coexist — in a rhythm that works for you.
