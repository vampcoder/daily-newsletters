But knowing better isn’t the same as doing better.

I still get pulled off track by the smallest things. I’ll sit down to do something important, and before I know it, I’m checking a message, opening a browser tab, or convincing myself that some small task is urgent enough to interrupt what I had planned to do.

I should've known better. But knowing better does not always mean doing.

Here are a few questions that have helped me on my journey, starting with identifying the real cause of distraction:

1. When was the last time you became aware of a distraction in your life? What did the distraction take from you?
2. What discomfort did you feel immediately before the distraction? (Fear, Overwhelm, Jealousy, Boredom, etc.)

Most distractions don’t come from external triggers, like our phones, inboxes, or notifications, but from internal triggers. Internal triggers are the uncomfortable feelings we try to escape, the ones you identified in question 2. Once you understand the discomfort behind the distraction, you can design a better response instead of relying on willpower.

Before I had a system to reclaim my time, I easily lost one to two hours every day to distraction. Over a week, that amounts to at least 7 hours—a full working day—that I could have devoted to my health, relationships, or the values I wanted my life to reflect.
