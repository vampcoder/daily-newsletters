I hope you're having a good summer! I've been meaning to do a newsletter sharing some thoughts on a few books I've read recently. Here are some books I found interesting:

## 1. Envy: A Theory of Social Behaviour by Helmut Schoeck

Part sociological theory, part political tirade, I found Schoeck's analysis of envy in society to be interesting and provocative. Schoeck argues that envy is caustic, and all functional societies learn to suppress it to some extent. By discouraging individual effort and achievement, envy holds back not just productivity and wealth, but even ordinary virtues as people don't want to attract the "evil eye" of their neighbors' envy.

Schoeck also argues that envy, as an emotion, doesn't function the way class-conscious political organizers would like it to. We are much more envious of small differences among our close associates than we are of large gaps between us and people far away. One can think of the Cultural Revolution in China where the brunt of the malice was borne not by ruling elites, but by schoolteachers, small landowners and other people just slightly better off than others in their local villages.

Although the targets of Schoeck's tirades against a politics built on envy are largely left-wing, it was hard not to read a similar sentiment into much of the right-wing populism sweeping the world today. Although we might like to think we've matured into a culture that can debate the trade-offs between growth and equality, or environment and economy, *Envy* is a potent reminder that, in many ways, we're still a society of children upset that the kid next door has a slightly nicer toy than we do.

## 2. Infinite Powers by Steven Strogatz

A fascinating book on the history of calculus. I really enjoy Strogatz's work, especially his class on chaos theory. This book, looking at the history of one of mathematics' crowning achievements is also great.

One minor quibble I have is that Strogatz seems almost to want to bend over backwards to convince the reader that calculus is important. It's almost as if a pushy editor told him that most people find calculus boring and to amp up the practical examples to maximize interest. I find it hard to believe that anyone whose eyes glaze over at the thought of advanced math would bother to read this book, so I find the efforts to hype the subject somewhat unnecessary.
