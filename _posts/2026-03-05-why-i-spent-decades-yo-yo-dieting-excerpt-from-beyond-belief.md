The real question isn't "Is this belief true?" but "Does this belief serve me?"

For much of my life, I was the kid who never took off his shirt at the community pool. While other teenagers splashed in the Central Florida heat, I'd sit on the edge wearing an oversized t-shirt to hide my belly rolls.

Over the next thirty years, my bookshelf became a graveyard of diet books. I meticulously logged fat grams in 1994. Three years later, I filled my fridge with tofu and potatoes. Then those same foods became contraband as I embraced keto. Eventually, intermittent fasting became my new gospel.

Each plan felt like the answer. And in a way, each one was. I'd lose weight, feel better, preach my latest revelation at parties.

But every time, something awful would happen. I'd read an article explaining why my current diet was wrong. As my confidence faltered, so did my results. New failure-justifying beliefs crept in: "It's hard for bigger people to exercise." "It doesn't matter what I eat."

The pounds crept back. Every diet worked... until it didn't.

**My Glass Walls**

It wasn't about finding the perfect plan. It was about belief.

When I truly believed in a diet—when I was convinced it would work—I followed through with devotion. But the moment doubt crept in, commitment collapsed.

What I didn't see then: A belief can be helpful without being universal or even strictly true. The real breakthrough came when I stopped chasing the "perfect" plan and started believing that consistent daily choices mattered more than giant overhauls.

Think of the last time you quit something that mattered. Maybe it was the novel gathering dust, the business idea you talked yourself out of, or the difficult conversation you've been avoiding.

What story did you tell yourself?

"I'm not creative enough." "It's too late." "People like me don't succeed at this."

We repeat these phrases until they feel true. But they're not facts. These invisible barriers are what keep us from persevering longer than we thought possible.

**The Missing Side of Motivation**

Most people picture motivation as a straight line: want the benefit, do the behavior. But this model is incomplete.

If knowing what to do were enough, we'd all follow through on everything good for us. You can have a perfect plan, but if you don't believe your effort will make a difference, you won't persist.

Motivation is a triangle. One side is behavior (actions you take). Another is benefit (outcomes you want). But at the base, holding everything together, is **belief**—your conviction that actions will lead to results.

All three are essential.
