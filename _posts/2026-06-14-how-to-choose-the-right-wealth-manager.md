# How to choose the right wealth manager

MFD, RIA, PMS, or broker—what's the difference, and which model fits you?

The options for managing your wealth are numerous. You can choose to invest yourself, or you can delegate the job to a professional. But even when you delegate, there are different types of experts, each with a different business model.

This article breaks down the four main types of financial intermediaries in India—**MFD**, **RIA**, **PMS**, and **broker**—and explains how they make money, what services they offer, and the pros and cons of each. Understanding the differences will help you pick the right partner for your financial journey.

---

## The main types of financial intermediaries

### 1. Mutual Fund Distributor (MFD)

A Mutual Fund Distributor (MFD) is an individual or entity registered with the Association of Mutual Funds in India (AMFI). Their primary role is to help you invest in mutual funds. They act as an intermediary between you and the mutual fund house.

**How do they make money?**

- **Commission from the fund house:** When you invest in a mutual fund through an MFD, the fund house pays them a commission. This commission is a percentage of the assets you invest, and it is built into the expense ratio of the fund.
- **Trail commission:** In addition to the upfront commission, MFDs also receive a trail commission as long as you stay invested. This is typically around 0.2% to 0.5% per year, depending on the fund category.

**Pros:**

- **Guidance:** MFDs provide guidance on which mutual funds to select, which is helpful for first-time investors.
- **Convenience:** They handle the paperwork and transactions for you.

**Cons:**

- **Potential conflict of interest:** Since MFDs earn commissions from fund houses, their advice can be biased toward funds that offer higher commissions rather than those that are best for you.
- **Limited to mutual funds:** They cannot advise on stocks, bonds, or other investment products.
- **No guarantee:** Unlike bank fixed deposits, mutual fund investments do not offer guaranteed returns.

---

### 2. Registered Investment Advisor (RIA)

A Registered Investment Advisor (RIA) is an individual or firm registered with the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI) to provide investment advice. They act as a fiduciary, meaning they are legally obligated to act in your best interest.

**How do they make money?**

- **Advisory fee:** RIAs typically charge you a fee for their advice. This can be a flat fee or a percentage of your portfolio (for example, 1% per annum).
- **No commissions:** Unlike MFDs, RIAs cannot earn commissions from fund houses or brokers.

**Pros:**

- **Objective advice:** Because they are fiduciaries and do not earn commissions, their advice is more likely to be unbiased.
- **Comprehensive planning:** They can provide holistic financial planning, including tax, insurance, and estate planning.

**Cons:**

- **Fee burden:** The advisory fee can eat into your investment returns, especially if your portfolio is small.
- **Minimum investment:** Many RIAs require a minimum portfolio size (often ₹1 crore or more).

---

### 3. Portfolio Management Service (PMS)

A Portfolio Management Service (PMS) is a professional service that manages a portfolio of stocks, bonds, and other securities on your behalf. PMS providers are registered with SEBI and are typically meant for large investors.

**How do they make money?**

- **Fixed fee:** PMS providers charge a fixed management fee, usually 2% to 2.5% per annum of the portfolio value.
- **Performance fee:** Some PMS providers also charge a performance fee—a share of the profits generated above a certain benchmark.

**Pros:**

- **Professional management:** Your money is managed by seasoned fund managers who have access to in-depth research and analysis.
- **Direct equity exposure:** PMS can provide more tailored investment strategies, including direct stock picking, which may not be possible through mutual funds.

**Cons:**

- **High fees:** The combination of fixed and performance fees can be substantial, potentially reducing your net returns.
- **High minimum investment:** PMS typically require a minimum investment of ₹50 lakh to ₹1 crore.

---

### 4. Broker

A broker is a member of a stock exchange who buys and sells securities on your behalf. Brokers can be full-service (offering research and advisory) or discount brokers (offering low-cost execution only).

**How do they make money?**

- **Brokerage fee:** Brokers charge a fee per trade—either a fixed amount or a percentage of the trade value.
- **Other charges:** Some brokers may also charge for research reports, advisory services, or account maintenance.

**Pros:**

- **Direct control:** You have full control over investment decisions.
- **Low cost for discount brokers:** Discount brokers offer very low brokerage rates for self-directed investors.

**Cons:**

- **No advice (for discount brokers):** Discount brokers do not provide investment advice; you must do your own research.
- **Risk of overtrading:** With access to a trading platform, you might be tempted to trade too frequently, which can erode returns due to brokerage and taxes.

## How to choose the right model?

The right choice depends on your financial situation, investment knowledge, and the level of involvement you want.

| **If you...** | **You might consider...** |
|:---|:---|
| Are new to investing and want help selecting mutual funds | An **MFD** or **RIA** |
| Have a large portfolio and want professional equity management | A **PMS** |
| Prefer to manage your own investments at a low cost | A **discount broker** |
| Want comprehensive financial planning | An **RIA** |

**Key considerations:**

- **Transparency:** Always ask how the advisor is compensated—by you or by the product provider.
- **Track record:** Check the advisor's credentials, experience, and regulatory standing.
- **Costs:** Understand all fees—upfront, ongoing, and performance-based—and how they impact your net returns.
- **Regulatory oversight:** Ensure the intermediary is registered with the appropriate regulatory body (SEBI, AMFI, etc.).

Ultimately, the best wealth manager is one who aligns with your financial goals, charges fairly, and acts in your best interest. Take the time to compare options and ask questions before committing.
